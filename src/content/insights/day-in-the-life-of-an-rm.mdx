In commercial banking, the Relationship Manager (RM) sits at the centre of growth. They are the frontline of revenue generation, customer engagement, and portfolio expansion. Yet, beneath this critical role lies a structural inefficiency that is quietly diluting sales effectiveness.

A typical day for an RM rarely begins with strategy or client acquisition. Instead, it starts with reviewing pending actions—following up with customers for documentation, coordinating with credit teams on proposal status, aligning with operations for processing updates, and working with internal teams to refine submissions. Throughout the day, proposals are built, reworked, and routed across systems for approvals, often requiring multiple iterations and manual interventions.

By the time the day ends, a significant portion of effort has gone into coordination rather than conversion.

The reality is stark: a large share of an RM's time—often upwards of 80%—is consumed by non-revenue activities. This includes navigating multiple systems such as LOS, LMS, CBS, and CRM, reconciling fragmented borrower and exposure data, managing manual workflows between business, credit, and operations, and handling extensive documentation requirements across the lending lifecycle.

None of these inefficiencies are individually catastrophic. But collectively, they create a silent drag on productivity.

This is the core issue: sales performance in banking is rarely broken by one major gap. It is slowed down by hundreds of small inefficiencies that accumulate over time. The RM, who should be focused on deepening relationships, increasing wallet share, and identifying new opportunities, is instead operating as a coordinator within a fragmented ecosystem.

The consequence is not just reduced productivity—it is lost revenue potential. Delayed decision-making, slower turnaround times, and limited customer engagement ultimately impact competitiveness in a market where speed and experience are becoming key differentiators.

The opportunity, however, is equally clear.

Banks do not necessarily need large-scale, disruptive transformations to address this challenge. What is required is a systematic reduction of friction across the lending lifecycle—enabling faster decision-making, providing unified visibility into borrower exposures, and minimizing operational dependencies.

When marginal inefficiencies are addressed at scale, the cumulative impact can be significant. Even incremental improvements in turnaround time, data accessibility, and workflow efficiency can meaningfully shift how RMs allocate their time.

Reimagining the RM's day is not about adding more tools—it is about removing friction. It is about ensuring that the primary driver of the sales engine is empowered to do what they do best: build relationships, understand clients, and drive revenue.

Because in the end, growth in commercial banking is not constrained by opportunity—it is constrained by how effectively that opportunity is pursued.
